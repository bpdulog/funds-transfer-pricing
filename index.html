Modern Banking • Matched-Maturity FTP

Central Treasury as an Internal Clearinghouse

Neither the branch manager nor the loan officer touches duration risk. Central Treasury buys funding from the branch and sells it to the loan desk at market-matched curve rates.

Branch Desk: Pays 1.00%, sells to Treasury at 3.50% → +2.50% Franchise Spread
Lending Desk: Buys at 3.50% from Treasury, lends at 5.25% → +1.75% Credit Spread
Treasury Desk: Absorbs the term gap & hedges it via swaps.
When Fed Hikes +300 bps: Both Desk Margins Remain 100% Protected!

Central Treasury & Asset-Liability Management (ALM)

Acts as the internal bank of the bank. It buys wholesale liquidity from deposit branches at the matched market rate and lends it to commercial loan desks. It centralizes all interest rate duration risk and hedges it in financial markets.

Core Concepts

Essential Questions in Funds Transfer Pricing

How leading financial institutions, treasurers, and risk committees manage complex balance-sheet dynamics.

In a bank without FTP, branches that gather deposits are treated as "cost centers" because they generate expenses (branch rent, tellers, ATMs) and pay interest, while lending desks look like "superstar profit centers" because they book big loan coupons.

FTP fixes this distorted incentive. When a branch gathers $10 million in low-cost checking accounts, Treasury "buys" that funding at the market rate (e.g. 4.10%). The branch earns a credit of 4.10% − 1.25% paid = +2.85% pure franchise profit. Branch managers are fairly rewarded for bringing sticky, low-cost liquidity to the bank.

Checking and consumer savings accounts are Non-Maturity Deposits (NMDs). Technically, customers can withdraw their balance in 5 seconds on their phone.

However, statistically, a large portion of retail deposits never leaves the bank—they are "sticky core deposits." Banks use Behavioral Replication Portfolios:

  • Volatile / Operational Tranche (e.g., 20%): Modeled as 1-month to 3-month overnight funds.
  • Core Sticky Tranche (e.g., 80%): Modeled as a moving average ladder of 3-year, 5-year, and 7-year maturities.

This allows the bank to credit branches with a higher, longer-term FTP rate that reflects the true economic stability of core deposits.

A standard yield curve only measures pure risk-free interest rates (like U.S. Treasuries or SOFR). But in the real banking system:

  • Term Liquidity Premium (LP): Long-term funding is scarce and expensive during crises. Treasury charges loans a premium for tying up bank cash for 5, 10, or 30 years.
  • Basel III LCR / NSFR Charges: Regulations mandate that banks hold High-Quality Liquid Assets (HQLA) against volatile deposits and illiquid loans. Treasury assigns explicit FTP charges or credits based on how each asset or liability impacts regulatory ratios.

Silicon Valley Bank received over $100 billion in surge venture tech deposits between 2020 and 2021. Because tech startups didn't need many loans, SVB invested those deposits into 10-year and 30-year fixed-rate Treasury bonds and agency mortgage-backed securities earning ~1.6%.

The Fatal Error: SVB treated these surge deposits as permanent, stable money without properly charging the investment portfolio for the massive duration mismatch or putting on payer interest rate swaps. When the Fed hiked rates to 5%, the market value of those fixed bonds plummeted by $17+ billion, wiping out SVB's tangible common equity and triggering a fatal $42 billion bank run.

A rigorous FTP system would have penalized the purchase of 10-year assets funded by tech deposits by charging a heavy duration transfer rate, forcing the bank to hedge or turn away unprofitable flighty deposits.
